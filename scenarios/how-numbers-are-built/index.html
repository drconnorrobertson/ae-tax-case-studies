How the Numbers in the Scenarios Are Built

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

Scenario Library / About the Scenario Library / How the Numbers in the Scenarios Are Built
Illustrative scenario. This page describes a composite hypothetical created for education. It is not a real client, and no real person or business is described. Figures are simplified assumptions, not results, and no outcome is guaranteed or typical.

Worked examples are helpful only if you understand how they were built. This page explains the conventions behind the arithmetic in the scenarios and shows how to adapt an example with your own numbers. It also explains why the library prefers assumptions to averages.

Why Assumptions Instead of Averages

A scenario needs numbers to show how a rule works, but the numbers should not suggest that they are typical. Averages can imply that a result is common, and the library does not have data to support any such claim. Instead, each scenario uses assumptions chosen for clarity. If a scenario says a study reclassifies 20 percent of a building's basis, that number is an input, not a finding. Change it to 10 percent or 35 percent and the arithmetic changes accordingly.

Round Numbers

The scenarios use round figures such as 100,000 dollars or 250,000 dollars so that the steps are easy to follow. Some computed figures, such as self-employment tax, are not round, and the scenarios show them as computed, often rounded to the nearest dollar. Where a figure is described as about a certain amount, it has been rounded.

Flat Rates

Real tax systems use graduated brackets, phaseouts, and multiple layers of tax. To keep the arithmetic readable, scenarios often use a flat assumed marginal rate, such as 24 percent or 32 percent. A flat rate is a simplification. It can overstate or understate the tax in a particular case. When a scenario uses one, it says so and usually notes what was ignored, such as the standard deduction, state tax, and the qualified business income deduction.

Common Simplifications

Scenarios typically simplify in several ways, and each one is flagged where it matters:

  • Ignoring the first-year convention. Real property depreciation uses a mid-month convention, and personal property uses conventions that reduce first-year amounts. Many examples show a full year.
  • Ignoring state tax. State rules differ widely, so the arithmetic is mostly federal.
  • Ignoring phaseouts. Deductions and credits often phase out at higher incomes. Examples may not model the phaseout.
  • Ignoring the time value of money. Comparisons of timing typically show totals without discounting.
  • Treating limits as assumptions. Contribution limits and thresholds are stated as assumptions.

Building Your Own Version

You can adapt an example using your own numbers. A helpful sequence is:

  1. Write down the inputs the scenario uses, such as income, cost, holding period, and rate.
  2. Replace each with your own figure or your best estimate.
  3. Follow the same steps of arithmetic.
  4. Note which steps depend on rules that you are unsure about, and mark them as questions.
  5. Bring the result and the questions to your advisor.

The result is not a plan. It is an organized starting point for a conversation.

Checking the Arithmetic

Each scenario's arithmetic is intended to be checkable. If you rebuild an example from its stated assumptions and get a different number, that may be a sign of rounding, of a step the scenario omitted, or of a mistake. Small differences from rounding are expected.

Sensitivity Testing

A good habit is to change one assumption at a time and see how the result moves. If a small change flips the answer, the decision is fragile and deserves more analysis. If the answer holds across a reasonable range, it is more robust. Many scenarios include a section on variations, which does this informally.

What the Numbers Cannot Capture

Numbers cannot capture the quality of records, the risk of examination, the reliability of a buyer, or the intangible costs of complexity. These factors can be as important as the arithmetic. Scenarios discuss them in words for that reason.

Keeping Current

Rates, limits, and rules change. If you use a scenario to plan, replace each assumption with the current figure from an official source or your advisor. Do not treat the assumed rate or limit as current law.

Questions to Bring to Your Advisor

  1. Which of my inputs are different from the scenario's assumptions?
  2. Which simplifications matter most for me?
  3. What would a more complete calculation include?
  4. How sensitive is my result to a change in my assumptions?

A Worked Rebuild as an Example

Suppose a scenario computes a payroll tax figure by multiplying a salary by 15.3 percent. To adapt it, replace the salary with your own figure, confirm whether the wage base applies to your income, and check whether any part of your pay is exempt. Then compare the result with the scenario's figure and note the difference. If the difference is large, look at which input caused it. That habit teaches you which assumptions matter most, and it gives your advisor a precise place to begin.

Where to Find Current Figures

For current-year limits, thresholds, and rates, consult official IRS publications and your state tax agency, or ask your advisor to confirm them. Numbers change each year, and some change during a year when legislation is enacted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do scenarios use flat tax rates?

To keep the arithmetic readable. A flat rate is a simplification, and real results depend on brackets, phaseouts, and state tax.

Are the percentages in the scenarios typical?

No. They are assumptions chosen for illustration, not averages or benchmarks.
