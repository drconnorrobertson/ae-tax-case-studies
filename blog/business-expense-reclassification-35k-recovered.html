Business Expense Reclassification Recovers $35,000

The Client

Andrea Collins operated a marketing agency in Charlotte, North Carolina, generating $380,000 in annual revenue with $245,000 in net income. She had four employees and worked from a dedicated home office. Her annual federal tax burden was $62,000.

The Problem

Andrea's prior tax preparer had been conservatively categorizing numerous deductible business expenses as personal -- including her dedicated home office ($8,400/year), business vehicle use ($11,200/year), professional development ($4,800/year), client entertainment ($3,600/year adjusted for 50% limitation), and business insurance ($2,400/year). Over three years, $91,200 in legitimate business deductions had gone unclaimed.

Our Strategy

We performed a thorough expense review across three years of bank and credit card statements, reclassifying expenses that met IRS deductibility requirements. We verified business purpose for each category -- measured the home office, calculated actual vehicle mileage logs, confirmed professional development was industry-related, and documented client entertainment with business purpose notations. We then filed amended returns for all three years.

$35,000
Total Recovered Over 3 Years

The Results

The three amended returns recovered $35,000 in overpaid taxes by properly deducting $91,200 in business expenses that had been improperly categorized as personal. Andrea also implemented a bookkeeping system to ensure all deductible expenses are captured going forward, saving an estimated $12,000 per year in ongoing tax reduction.

Key Takeaway

Conservative tax preparation is not the same as accurate tax preparation. Business owners who are told their home office, vehicle, or professional development expenses are not deductible should get a second opinion -- legitimate business expenses are not optional deductions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What business expenses are most commonly missed?

Home office deductions, business vehicle mileage, professional development and continuing education, business insurance, professional subscriptions, and client-related travel and entertainment are the most frequently overlooked business deductions.

How do I document business expenses for IRS compliance?

Keep receipts, maintain a mileage log, note the business purpose for meals and entertainment, measure your home office space, and use a separate business bank account or credit card. Contemporaneous records are the best defense in an audit.
